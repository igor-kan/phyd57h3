 <h2> Problem 1.1 [40 pts] F-16 crash on 28.08.2025
</h2>
<img width=330 src="https://planets.utsc.utoronto.ca/~pawel/pix/Loop.png"> 
 <p>

A fatal accident occured on 28.08.2025 during preparations to the annual airshow
in Radom, Poland. Polish F-16 fighter jet piloted by Maj. Maciej Krakowian was 
finishing a loop that he started from about 30 m above the runway. On return, 
the plane collided with the runway and burst into a fireball. The wind was 
negligible and the visibility unlimited. On 3 September, during an official 
visit to Washigton D.C. of the Polish president, four F-35 fighters flew over 
the White House, followed by the 
<a href="https://www.aol.com/watch-missing-man-flyover-honor-181329973.html">
missing man formation</a> of four F-16s (maneuver often seen at commemorations,
where one of the aircraft departs from the 3 others and flies toward the sky 
along a partial loop).  
<p>
A significant amount of photo evidence such as this graphic video:
<a href="https://planets.utsc.utoronto.ca/~pawel/pix/F-16-28.08.2025.mp4"> 
F-16-28.08.2025.mp4 </a> 
suggests that nothing was wrong with the plane and the accident may have been 
due to pilot's error. The investigation is ongoing, no official cause of the 
accident has yet been announced. 
<p> 
It is up to you to perform the analytical and then semi-numerical 
modelling of the idealized, perfectly circular loop. The real trajectory was 
not perfectly closed and thus crossed the runway surface, but the non-closure 
i.e. difference of loop radii on ascent and descent relative to average 
was likely only 1:20, relatively small. Many valid insights follow from 
assuming, for simplicity, that the path was a perfect circle of radius R.
<p> 
Perform a physical analysis of the crash following the provided guidelines
and answer all the questions asked. This will enable yoassig1-D57-2026-sol.htmlu to, in
the conclusions section, express your expert opinion as to whether the pilot 
could have been incapacitated by g-forces, whether the plane stalled, and so on. 


<h3> Oversimplified (analytical) model </h3>

In this model, you will assume that the pilot steers the aircraft to produce
a lift force allowing its center of mass to follow a circular path of radius 
$R$, and that the variable air drag force on the plane (air drag depends on its 
airspeed and that the angle of attack of the wing) is always perfectly matched 
by the opposing, variable engine thrust. Effective drag is thus zero. <br>

This is a nice analytical (i.e., non-numerical) model and only partly 
unrealistic. Loop's circulatity is a goal of competition aerobatics (judges 
score a figure to visually assess it). But the drag=thrust situation is 
very difficult to achieve in practice (no pilot or mechanism can control thrust
so precisely). We will consider a more realistic situation later. 
<p>
Use (i) energy conservation principle, (ii) assumption that a loop of radius 
R=const. results at its highest point in zero g-force felt by the pilot 
(weigthlessness). 
<p>
Show that the radius R and airspeed $v$ at every point of the trajectory are a 
function of the initial speed $v_0$. Find the formulae. You can express 
position of a point on a circle by angle $\phi$ it forms with the downward
vertical line beginning at the center of the circle. 
<p>
Evaluate $R$, the speed $v_1$ at the top of the loop, and the load factor $n_0$
 (acceleration pushing the pilot into the seat, in units of $g$ = 9.81 
 m/s<sup>2</sup>) at the bottom of the loop, assuming the typical entry speed 
 $v_0$ = 180 m/s = 350 knots,
recommended for the F-16. 
<br>Load factors $-6 < n < 9$ experienced for up to several 
seconds are not considered risky for the trained and well-equipped military 
pilot. Higher overload may lead to greyout, tunnel vision, or even loss
of conciousness (so-called g-lock). Was the pilot in the accident aircraft 
in any danger due to g-forces, assuming this model of aircraft dynamics?  
<p>
Can you figure out the time $T$ needed to perform the described loop?
Hint: $$T = \oint dt = R \oint \frac{d\phi}{v(\phi)}.$$  


<h4> <font color=magenta> Solution </font></h4>

Energy conservation: $$\frac{E}{M} = \frac{v^2}{2} + g\,h(\phi) = 
\frac{v_0^2}{2} = const.$$
where 
$$ h(\phi) = R\,(1-\cos \phi), $$
results in 
$$v^2 = v_0^2 - 2gR\,(1-\cos \phi).$$

The load factor (g-factor) is the sum of centrifugal and gravity component 
accelerations, divided by $g = 9.81$ m/s$^2$: 
$$ n(\phi) = \frac{v^2}{gR} + \cos \phi \,.$$
Condition of weightlessness at the top: $n(\phi=\pi) = v_1^2/(gR) - 1 =  0$, 
gives $ v_1^2 = gR$. Since, at the top of the loop, by one of the previous
equations $v_1^2 = v_0^2 - 4gR$, we obtain
$$ v_0^2 = 5 gR = 5 v_1^2,$$
or
$$ R = \frac{v_0^2}{5g}.$$
<p>
With this, the formula for airspeed simplifies to 
 $$v(\phi) = \sqrt{gR\,(3+2\cos \phi)} =  v_1\,\sqrt{3+2\cos \phi} = 
 v_0 \sqrt{\frac{1}{5}(3+2\cos \phi)}.$$
 <br>
Numerically, if $v_0 = 350 $ kt = 180 m/s, then $R=660.5$ m, and $v_1 = 
v_0/\sqrt{5} = 80.5$ m/s = 156.5 kt. 
<p>
We see that the largest g-factor is 6 at the bottom of the loop (at the 
very beginning and very end). This would be quite tolerable by the pilot. 
<br>
As to the possible danger of a stall, it's not there either. Here is the 
squared ratio of $v(\phi)$ to stall speed $v_S(\phi)$: 
$$ \frac{v^2(\phi)}{v_S^2(\phi)} = \frac{v_1^2}{(110 \mbox{kt})^2}
\;\frac{3+2 \cos \phi}{3(1+\cos \phi)}$$ 
that is reaching a minimum at $\phi=0$. The minimum value is equal $v(0)/v_S(0) 
= 1.30.$ In other words, the entry and exit speeds are 1.3 or more times the 
stall speed, in our idealized loop model. It's no coincidence that a factor 
of precisely 1.3 is used in aviation as a speed safety margin, for instance 
when a recomended, safe, approach speed is calculated for a rather slowly flying
airplane on final approach to landing. It's the same factor here. That's why 
pilots of F-16 are taught to normally enter a loop at the airspeed of 350 kt!
<p>
Finally, one can calculate the full time to close the loop
$$ T =  R\;\oint \frac{d\phi}{v(\phi)} =
 2\,\sqrt{\frac{R}{g}} \;\int_0^\pi \frac{d\phi}{\sqrt{3+2\cos \phi}} =
4\,\sqrt{\frac{R}{5g}} K(4/5)$$
where $K(k)$ is the elliptic integral of the 1st kind.
  $$ T \simeq 4.0378 \,\sqrt{\frac{R}{g}} \simeq 33.1 s$$
If you are not familiar with elliptic integrals, notice that 
the integrated fraction has the following expansion 
$$ T = 2\,\sqrt{\frac{R}{3g}} \;\int_0^\pi
\left(1 - \frac{\cos\phi}{3}+ \frac{\cos^2\phi}{6} -\frac{5\cos^3\phi}{54}
+\frac{35\cos^4\phi}{648}+...\right ) \;{d\phi}  =$$
$$ = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{R}{3g}} 
\left(1 +\frac{1}{12} -\frac{35}{288}+... \right ) \simeq 4.0034
 \,\sqrt{\frac{R}{g}} \simeq 32.9 \,s. $$
Not a bad approximation, despite $\cos \phi$ not being, in general, 
$\ll 1$. Our idealized model is not very precise about the timing of the actual
loop ($T\approx 27$ s); it's a consequence of the thrust=drag assumption plus 
the weightlessness assumption at the top. Let's do a little better. 


<h3> 1-D numerical model </h3>

Relaxing the assumption of the effectively frictionless motion along the loop, 
consider the forces acting along the circular loop trajectory. The first is
gravity. It provides acceleration $f_g = -g \, \sin \phi$. 
<p>
Next, the engine thrust. In the movie of the accident linked above, a 
bright streak behind the engine (flame from the afterburner) is seen 
<i>steadily</i>, throughout the whole loop. We will assume the thrust from the 
jet engine was constant. Pratt & Whitney jet engine has a large maximum thrust
force used for takeoff, but during aerobatics it is normally set lower;
we will assume $F = 9.5$ tonnes, or 9500 kG = 93200 N. Knowing that the
mass of the F-16 was likely $M=15000$ kg, the thrust force $F$, the plane's 
unempeded acceleration becomes $f_t = F/M = 6.213$ m/s$^2 = 0.633$ g. 
<p>
Finally, there is air drag acceleration $f_d$ that depends, in some way, on 
the angle of attack and the square of the airspeed (form drag), because the 
drag is a sum of parasitic or form drag, and the induced drag. Use this simple
ansatz
$$f_d = - \left(\frac{4 + n}{5}\right)\; C_D\, \frac{A}{M}\, 
\frac{\rho v^2}{2}$$ where $n$ is the instantaneous load factor (along the line
between the aircraft and loop's center), $C_D = 0.1$ is nondimensional drag 
coefficient of F-16, $A=28$ m$^2$ is the wing area, and the density of air is
$\rho = 1.22$ kg/m$^3$. The expression in parentheses is effectively doubling
the coefficient of drag ($C_D$) when g-forces grow from $n=1$ to $n=6$. 
<p>
You can use the same entry speed $v_0$ and $R$ as in analytical model. 
Carefully set up a second-order differential equation for the position angle 
$\phi$ of the aircraft, checking the signs and units of all the terms in at 
least two different characteristic points of the trajectory. Angle $\phi$ 
starts at $\phi_0 = 0$, is $\phi = \phi_1 = \pi$ at the top of the loop, 
and $\phi_2 = 3 \pi/2$ at the point where the plane flies down vertically, 
becoming $\phi = 2\pi$ at the end of the loop.
<br>
Also write an equation for $n(\phi)$. The centrifugal acceleration
formula $a_r = v^2/R$ will help you in this. This part produced a positive 
load factor. Just don't forget that the g-force or load factor $n$ is a sum of: 
component of gravity's $\vec{g}$ along the position radius $\vec{R}$ 
(that component changes not only value but also sign, since the pilot's body 
is pushed by gravity into the seat at the loop's bottom, while pulled away 
from the seat at top of the loop).  That's all. Total acceleration of motion 
along its path is $d^2s/dt^2 = R \,d^2\phi/dt^2$, so you'll have a 2nd-order 
ODE for the position (angle) of the airplane.  
<p>
Since, unlike in the oversimplified model, there are no easy tricks 
(energy conservation law), and the equation is non-linear in $v$, it is 
impossible to solve it analytically, and you must resort to numerical 
integration of the equation of motion in time, breaking it off when $\phi$
reaches $2\pi$. Explain and use any numerical method you know, the simpler the 
less time you'll spend debugging. A short time step of integration $dt=0.001$ s
will guarantee a good accuracy of the result. 
 <p>

 <h2> Problem 1.2 [25 pts] Ballistics in air and water </h2>
 
$$ \ddot{\vec{r}} = -g\, \hat{y} -\frac{C_d A}{2M} \rho\, v \; \vec{v}$$
It's a standard exterior ballistics problem, 
but the optimum angle is a function of the parameters (diameter and mass) 
of the bullet and the initial muzzle speed always taken as 710 m/s.
It requires some computation to reach the required accuracy of the best-range
angle of 0.005 degree, but not so much that Python cannot handle it. 
 

<h4> <font color=magenta> Solution </font></h4>

The solution is based on output from <a href="bullet_2026.py"> this program</a>.
<p>
The script uses the simplest method possible (2nd order accuracy leapfrog with 
timestep dt=0.002 s). Notice that high-order methods are not needed in view of 
the generally short running time. However, there is a thing which can make 
the code very slow: graphics done during calculation. Graphics is typically 
handled by one CPU thread. We avoid storing all points on trajectories, 
opting to plot them as soon as they are calculated. This is memory-efficient 
but slow (die to overhead of pyplot.scatter(x,y) instruction). But in the above 
program, one out of every 50 points is plotted, which speeds up the runtime, 
and very nicely shows the changing speed of the projectile as variable point 
spacing. Ten times smaller spacing means ten times smaller speed.

<h2> Problem 1.3 [22 pts] Subinterval with maximum growth of value </h2>

This problem comes up in the analysis of time series, such as financial data
(where clients are actually more concerned with the maximum drop than increase
of value but it's an equivalent problem), and in the physical sciences (e.g., 
finding the biggest voltage drop measured along some path). 
<p>
You are given a series of values $x$ of length $N$; that is a vector of values
$x_i$, where $i=1...N$. 


Formulate an algorithm that finds the maximum increase of $x$ (representing 
investment or voltage) in some contiguous segment of the data, i.e. the largest
increase of value from some value $x_i$ to a higher value $x_j$ at index $j > i$. 
<p>
For instance, if the series is $x$ = {8, 9, 6, 7, 8, 7, 11, 9, 8, 8, 1, 4, 2} 
then the largest increase is between positions $i=3$ and $j=7$, and equals 
$x_j-x_i= 11-6=5$. Notice that we could have even more points between those 
two, with value somewhere between 6 and 11, and the resulting maximum increase 
would not change. A different $j$ will be obtained but we are not interested 
in either $i$ or $j$ themselves, only in the maximum change (location of the
interval of interest can be provided with very little extra work, but our
problem does not ask for it). Your algorithm must thus somehow 
not be overly focused on local extrema.
<p>
You can describe your algorithm in words, and/or implement it, commenting the
program very well, so it is clear how it works and how you proved to yourself 
that it works correctly.
<p>
Remark: Since all contiguous subsets (subintervals) of the dataset provide 
possible solution, examining them all separately would take an effort of 
computational complexity $O(N^2)$, because the number of all index pairs 
{$j\gt i$} is $N(N-1)/2$, so $\sim N^2$. For large $N$ this is an awful lot of
computation. Try to obtain a cheaper asymptotic scaling with $N$ if possible. 
If you can't, code an inefficient method scaling like $N^2$, for a partial
credit of up to 13 pts. 

<h4> <font color=magenta> Solution </font></h4>

This is essentially another problem in disguise. Imagine that you have found
the subinterval with maximum difference at endpoints,  and consider replacing 
the original series with first difference series (value at a point minus the 
value at the previous point), which is similar to numerical differentiation. 
Then the found subinterval will correspond to a subinterval in the 
differenced series with a maximum sum of values. In design and analysis of 
algorithms, this kind of problem is called a maximum subarray problem.  
<br>
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximum_subarray_problem">
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximum_subarray_problem </a>
<p>
The problem has an $O(N)$ solution, much faster than the simplest $O(N^3)$
solution consisting in checking all contiguous subintervals of a 1-D set of 
numbers. [Yes, it's that bad, if you use series of differences, because there
are of order $O(N^2)$ subintervals, and each requires of order $O(N)$ additions
to do the sum within subinterval.]  
<p>
Every time-series or a 1-D spatial scan of physical variables can be easily
differentiated numerically, but what about the length of the differenced series? 
Isn't it smaller by 1? That would mean we lose some information. The 
differencing will preserve all the information and be reversible if we use the 
same amount of memory (N-1 differences plus 1 starting value of the original 
series in the first memory location = N values). Let's call the differentiated 
array 'numbers'. 

<pre>
def max_subarray(numbers):
    """Find the largest sum of any contiguous subarray."""
    best_sum = float('-inf')
    current_sum = 0
    for x in numbers:
        current_sum = max(x, current_sum + x)
        best_sum = max(best_sum, current_sum)
    return best_sum
</pre>

So that could be used to write a max growth program, except that this would 
be counter to the spirit of self-sufficient programming. Let us be more 
ambitious and create our own algorithm that does not involve the above method 
(differentiation + solving max subarray problem). 
<p>
Here is a good analogy to visualize. Think of the varying value of assets 
or amount of money in some investment account, as a function of discrete time, 
$X(t_n)$ or $X_n$. The task is to find a contiguous subinterval with the biggest
increase of account holdings. You start at the beginning of the series and 
assume for a while that this is where the optimal subinterval begins. 
Store in some variable the <b>starting value</b>, you can call it $x_0$. 
If the holdings initially increase, you continue, and just wait to see how big a
jump you can record. You track the <b>biggest increase encountered so far</b>, 
i.e. $\Delta = \max (X_n - x_0)$.  
The values of $X$ will sometimes reverse and go down for a while, fluctuate up
and down, but you ignore these local extrema, with one notable exception.
<br> 
When you encounter a value $X_n$ which is <i>lower</i> than $x_0$,  
well that may change the situation! It gives you a chance of recording (later)
a bigger jump from that lower trough, if the values go high enough, which is
not known in this single pass through the data --  but you must act 
nevertheless. Overwrite $x_0$ with a lower $X_n$, and continue
counting $\Delta$'s with the updated $x_0$. 
You also store somewhere (say, in variable $\Delta_{best}$)
the <b>best (most positive) $\Delta$</b> encountered so far, 
just in case you actually can't find any better $\Delta$ in the rest 
of the data. In the end, you will have scannned the data just once, $O(N)$ work.  
<p>
There is a weaker but still somewhat interesting algo - in the worst case it is
still almost as inefficient as the simplest one (checking all subintervals) 
since it is still $O(N^2)$, but in practice 
it should be much faster than literally finding all subintervals among $N$ 
points; the method typically will have a much better coefficient in front of 
$N^2$. You first scan the data in $O(N)$ time and forget all individual data, 
except the local extrema (and their indices). This typically reduces the number
of points considerably! 
Paradoxically, focusing on, instead of ignoring, local extrema already should 
help. How much? Depends on how choppy the original series is. If it's very 
noisy, then there will be lots of local extrema and the speedup will be so-so, 
but for smoother data, it should be a big help.


<h2> Problem 1.4 [15 pts] 1-D stencil for curvature</h2>


<b>1.</b> 
Prove theoretically by using Taylor series expansion at different values of the 
argument of function $y(x)$, that the following, symmetrical numerical 
approximation to the second derivative $y^"(x) = d^2y/dx^2$,  
$$f = \frac{y(x+h) - 2 y(x) + y(x-h)}{h^2},$$
$h$ being a small variation of argument $x$ (small in the sense $|h|\ll x$), 
has a second-order accuracy. In other words, the deviation $|f(x,h)-y^"(x)|$ 
is proportional to $h^2$. (Assume that the function $y(x)$ has non-singular 
derivatives of all required orders.)

<p> 
<b>2.</b> 
As an illustration, show that numerically for a particular choice of function: 
$$ y(x) = e^{-x^2}+ e^{x-2} $$
around the point $x=0.5$. Make a plot of the absolute value of difference 
between $y^"$ and its estimate $f$, as a function of $h$. 
<br>
The plot should have a log-log scaling to reveal power-law dependencies
between the error and $h$. (What is the expected slope?)
The range of $h$ should be from	$10^{-7}$, to $10^{-1}$, the number of
computed points 600 or 800, and the power of 10 that produces $h$ (not $h$ itself) 
should cover the range (-7,-1) uniformly. Otherwise there will be a pileup of 
points toward one end of the log-log plot. 
<p>
How well does your analytical work explain the observed curve in large-enough-$h$
part of the plot?
<p>
<b>3.</b> 
How do you explain the behavior of the plot in the part covering very small $h$?
Can you develop some quantitative prediction for the upper envelope of that part? 
<br> Hint: 
The arithmetic accuracy is finite and has to do with how the machine represents
floating point numbers (and that may depend on laguage you use).
If number $\epsilon >0$ is smaller than "machine
epsilon" value, the result of $1 \pm \epsilon$ equals number 1, in a given 
floating point computer arithmetic; the relative accuracy of any of the 4 
operations (+,-,*,/) is then $O(\epsilon)$.

<h4> <font color=magenta> Solution </font></h4>

The solution follows from the Taylor series expansion 
$$y(x +\epsilon) = y(x) +\frac{y'(x) \epsilon}{1!} + \frac{y^"(x)\epsilon^2}{2!}
 + \,... = \sum_{k=0}^\infty\, \frac{y^{(k)}\,\epsilon^k}{k!} $$
We need to keep the terms up to and including 5th ($k=4$, some of you stopped at 
the 3rd!), and write 3 formulae for $\epsilon = \{-h, 0, +h\}$. [By the way, I 
hope you're duly impresed by the capability of modern HTML to include LaTex 
math, through the use of javascript. Cf. the <a href="script.txt">
.html script </a> of this page.]
<p>
Substitution into the $f(x,h) = ...\;$ formula yields:
$$ f(x,h) = y^"(x) +\frac{1}{12}\,y^{''''}(x) \,h^2 \;+\;...$$
which proves that the inaccuracy is $O(h^2)$, making the scheme 2nd order, as
advertised. <br>
The log-log plot of $|f(x,h)-y^"(x)|$ vs. $h$ should show the slope of +2, i.e.
error decreasing with decreasing spacing $h$ as $h^2$, down to a point where the
accuracy of additions and subtractions in the numerator is affected by the 
arithmetic roundoff limit. After that, further decrease of $h$ only 
<i>increases</i> the error, because the numerator is a few times machine 
epsilon $\epsilon$, while the denominator ($h^2$) quickly decreases with 
decreasing $h$.
<p>
Definition of machine epsilon is that $1+ \epsilon$ has the same floating point
representation as 1, in a given floating point arithmetic. In plain Python, 
double precision variables and constants are the only ones used, so 
$\epsilon \approx$ 2e-16.  [You can enter Python interactively and see it by 
performing: (1+small number)-1, decreasing the small number until the result
reads 0.0; this 'small number' is about $\epsilon \approx 2.2\cdot 10^{-16}$, 
typical for double precision floats that use 8 bytes for representation 
in Python, C(++) and Fortran. In contrast, single precision would give 
a few times 1e-7.] 
<p>
The error of the <i>numerator</i> of our approximator $f$ for $y^"$ cannot 
be much smaller in absolute value than $\epsilon$ (on average), 
no matter how small $h$ gets. It's a combination of respresentation and of
inexact arithmetic operations. We can expect that the sign of error varies 
from point to point. 
<p>
Taking numerator and denominator into account, our estimator's minimum error is 
$O(\epsilon/h^2)$. When $f(x,h)$ is much larger than that, we still get many 
accurate digits in the answer.  But the machine arithmetic and representationall
inaccuracy completely spoils the computation, even the 1st digit, when $h$ 
is less than the value at the intersection of two lines on a log-log plot: 
(y""/12)$h^2$ (the value of truncation error, i.e. analytically expected
estimation error) and $\epsilon/h^2)$ (estimator's noise). Because, in our case,
 $y^{''''}(1/2) = 3e^{-1/4}+e^{-3/2} = 2.56$, that intersection happens 
at $h^4 \approx (12\cdot 2.2/2.56) \; 10^{-16}$, or $h_{opt} = 
h \approx$ 1.8e-4. This is the optimal $h$, at which both types of errors are  
roughly $(y^{''''}/12)h^2 \approx $ 0.7e-8. Let's see if we got the right 
predictions, this time using numerical computations!
<p>
 
<p>

At smaller $h$, as we have predicted, the error of the estimator has a

At $h \ll h_{opt}$, especially at $h=10^{-7}$, the error of $f$ is seen to be 
sizeable, above $10^{-2}$, which is more than at $h=0.01$. 
There is no sense to use $h \gg h_{opt}$, or $h \ll h_{opt}$; both choices
unnecessarily spoil the accuracy, while saving neither time nor memory.
