Problem 1.1 [30 pts] Crash of the F-16 plane in Poland

A fatal accident occured on 28.08.2025 during preparations to the annual airshow in Radom, Poland. Polish F-16 fighter jet was finishing a loop that started only about 30 m above the runway. On return, the plane collided with the runway and burst into a fireball. The wind was negligible and the visibility unlimited. On 3 September, during an official visit to Washigton D.C. of the Polish president, four F-35 fighters flew over the White House, followed by the missing man formation of four F-16s (maneuver often seen at commemorations, where one of the aircraft departs from the 3 others and flies toward the sky along a partial loop).

Photographic evidence such as this graphic video: F-16-28.08.2025.mp4 suggests that nothing was wrong with the plane and the accident may have been due to pilot's error. The investigation is ongoing, no official cause of the accident has yet been announced.

Be among the first to analyze and clarify this accident. Perform the analytical and then semi-numerical modelling of the idealized, perfectly circular loop. The real trajectory was not perfectly closed and thus has intersected the runway, but the amount of non-closure, i.e. difference of loop radii on ascent and descent relative to the average, was likely only 1:20, thus quite small. Many valid insights follow from the simplifying assumption that the path was a perfect circle of radius R.

Perform a physical analysis of the crash following the provided guidelines and answer all the questions asked. This will enable you to, in the conclusions section, to express your expert opinion on whether the pilot could have been incapacitated by g-forces, whether the plane stalled, and so on.

Oversimplified (analytical) model

In this model, you will assume that the pilot steers the aircraft to produce a lift force allowing its center of mass to follow a vertical, circular path of radius R, and that the variable air drag force on the plane (air drag depends on its airspeed and the the angle of attack of the wing) is always perfectly matched by the opposing, variable engine thrust. Effective drag is thus zero.
This is a very nice analytical (i.e., non-numerical) model, but a situation very difficult to achieve in practice (no pilot or mechanism can control thrust so quickly and precisely). We will consider a more realistic situation later.

Use (i) energy conservation principle, (ii) assumption that a loop of radius R=const. results, at its highest point, in a zero net g-force felt by the pilot (weigthlessness).

Show that the radius R and airspeed $v$ at every point of the trajectory are a function of the initial speed $v_0$. Find the formulae. You can express the position of a point on a circle by the angle $\phi$ it forms with the downward vertical line beginning at the center of the circle.

Evaluate $R$, the speed $v_1$ at the top of the loop, and the load factor $n_0$ (acceleration pushing the pilot into the seat, in units of $g$ = 9.81 m/s2) at the bottom of the loop, assuming the typical entry speed $v_0$ = 180 m/s = 350 knots, recommended for the F-16.
Load factors $-6 < n < 9$ experienced for up to several seconds are not considered risky for the trained and well-equipped military pilot. Higher overload may lead to greyout, tunnel vision, or even loss of conciousness (so-called g-lock). Was the pilot in the accident aircraft in any danger due to g-forces, assuming this model of aircraft dynamics?

Can you figure out the time $T$ needed to perform the described loop? Hint: $$T = \oint dt = R \oint \frac{d\phi}{v(\phi)}.$$

Simple numerical model

You will study a loop of radius $R$ obtained in the analytical model. Relaxing the assumption of the effectively frictionless motion along the loop, consider the forces acting along the circular loop trajectory. The first is gravity. It provides acceleration $f_g = -g \, \sin \phi$.

Next, the engine thrust. In the movie of the accident linked above, a bright streak behind the engine (flame from the afterburner) is seen steadily, throughout the whole loop. We will assume the thrust from the jet engine was constant. Pratt & Whitney jet engine has a large maximum thrust force used for takeoff, but during aerobatics it is normally set lower; we will assume $F = 9.5$ tonnes, or 9500 kG $\approx$ 93200 N. Knowing that the mass of the F-16 was about $M=15000$ kg, the thrust force $F$, the plane's acceleration due to thrust becomes $f_t = F/M = 93.2/15$ m/s$^2$ = 6.213 m/s$^2 = 0.633 g$.

Finally, there is air drag acceleration $f_d$ that depends, in some way, on the angle of attack and the square of the airspeed (form drag), because the drag is a sum of parasitic or form drag, and the induced drag. Use simplified Ansatz $$f_d = -\left(\frac{4 + n}{5}\right)\; C_D\, \frac{A}{M}\, \frac{\rho v^2}{2}$$ where $n$ is the instantaneous load factor (along the line between the aircraft and loop's center), $C_D = 0.1$ is nondimensional drag coefficient of F-16, $A=28$ m$^2$ is the wing area, and the density of air is $\rho = 1.22$ kg/m$^3$. The expression in parentheses is doubling the coefficient of frontal drag ($C_D$), when g-forces grow from $n=1$ to $n=6$.

You can use the same entry speed $v_0$ as earlier. Carefully set up a second-order equation for the position angle $\phi$ of the aircraft, checking the signs of all the term in at least two different characteristic points of trajectory. Angle $\phi$ starts at $\phi_0 = 0$, is $\phi = \phi_1 = \pi$ at the top of the loop, $\phi_2 = 3 \pi/2$ at the point where the plane rushes toward the ground vertically, and becomes $\phi = 2\pi$ at the end of the loop.
Also write an equation for $n(\phi)$. The centrifugal acceleration formula $a_r = v^2/R$ will help you in this. This part produced a positive load factor. Just don't forget that the g-force or load factor $n$ is a sum of: component of gravity's $\vec{g}$ along the position radius $\vec{R}$ (that component changes not only value but also sign, since the pilot's body is pushed by gravity into the seat at the loop's bottom, while pulled away from the seat at the top of the loop). That's all. Total acceleration of motion along its path is $d^2s/dt^2 = R \,d^2\phi/dt^2$, so you'll have a 2nd-order ODE for the position (angle) of the airplane.

Since, unlike in the oversimplified analytical model, there are no easy tricks like the energy conservation law, and the equation is non-linear in $v$, it is impossible to solve it analytically, and you must resort to step-by-step numerical integration of the equation of motion in time, breaking it off when $\phi$ becomes $2\pi$. Explain and use any numerical method you know, the simpler the better, since it will take less time to debug. A short time step of integration $dt=0.001$ s will guarantee a good accuracy.

Questions to answer include:
(i) What is the value of $n_0$, is it in the danger zone for the pilot?
(ii) What is the value of $n_1$, load factor at the top? What is the speed and altitude there?

What is the total time of the loop $T$? How does it compare to the one inferred from the video of the loop? (You may want to make a small correction to $T$, due to an incomplete range of $\phi$ traversed in the video.)
(v) How does the oversimplified model stack up against the more realistic one?
(vi) Your own questions.

Draw conclusions from the model as to how much the pilot is affected by the g-forces, and how close to stall speed the plane is in a standard loop. Notice: stall speed of F-16 without flap extension, at $n=1$, is 110 kt (knots). It grows with $n$ in proportion to $\sqrt{n}$, so the full expression is $v_S=110\,\sqrt{n}$ kt.

Please print the most interesting quantities every 100 timesteps, and at the precise time when the aircraft passes the characteristic points denoted by the subscripts above.

Problem 1.2 [30 pts] Ballistics in air and water

It is shot at a varying angle $\alpha$ to horizon, ranging from 1 to 90 degrees from point $(x,y)=(0,0)$ located on the shore of a 10-meter deep lake. The wind is negligible and the air density is $\rho = 1.225$ kg/m$^3$.

At point $(x,y) = (R,0)$, where we will call $R$ the range in the air, the bullet hits the water and decelerates faster, eventually reaching $y=-10$ m at the bottom of the lake. Density of the environment jumps to 1000 kg/m$^3$ (water density). Another, minor, change is that effective gravity force decreases because 1/8 of it is cancelled in water by buoyancy (that value applies to a bullet, but not, e.g., to a tennis ball!). we shall assume that the nondimensional coefficient of drag $C_d= 0.35$ applies to both air and water; it is a factor in the following equation of motion $$ \ddot{\vec{r}} = -g\, \hat{y} -\frac{C_d A}{2M} \rho\, v \; \vec{v}$$ where: $\rho$ is the variable density of the environment, $\vec{v}$ is the velocity of the bullet, $v = |\vec{v}|$, $\hat{y}$ is a vertical versor pointing upward, $A$ is the cross sectional area of the object, $M $ is its mass, and $g = 9.81$ m/s$^2$ in the air, and effectively 7/8 of that value in water.

(iv) the air time in seconds,
(v) and the range in water $R_W$, counting along the $x$ direction from the water entry point.

A. If the motion takes place in vacuum, the problem has a simple analytical solution for (i)-(iii). Please derive it. It should be the same in both media, simple and accurate. We suggest a 2nd order method such a leapfrog, with constant timestep dt=0.003 s in the air, switching to dt=1e-4 in water. Code the problem yourself in Python (do not use any black-box packages, modules, or websites).

Problem 1.3 [24 pts] Subinterval with the largest growth of value

This problem comes up in the analysis of time series, such as financial data, and in the physical sciences (e.g., what is the biggest voltage change along some path).

You are given a series of values $x$ of length $N$; it is a vector of values $x_i$, where $i=1...N$. Formulate an algorithm that finds the maximum increase of $x$ (representing investment or voltage) in some contiguous segment of the data, i.e. the largest increase of value from some value $x_i$ to a higher value $x_j$ at index $j > i$.

For instance, if the series is $x$ = {8, 9, 6, 7, 8, 7, 11, 9, 8, 8, 1, 4, 2} then the largest increase is between positions $i=3$ and $j=7$, and equals $x_j-x_i= 11-6=5$. Notice that we could have even more points between those two, with value somewhere between 6 and 11, and the resulting maximum increase would not change. A different $j$ will be obtained but we are not interested in either $i$ or $j$ themselves, only in the maximum change (location of the interval of interest can be provided with very little extra work, but our problem does not ask for it). Your algorithm must thus somehow not be overly focused on local extrema.

You can describe your algorithm in words, and/or implement it, commenting the program very well, so it is clear how it works and how you proved to yourself that it works correctly.

Remark: Since all contiguous subsets (subintervals) of the dataset provide possible solution, examining them all separately would take an effort of computational complexity $O(N^2)$, because the number of all index pairs {$j\gt i$} is $N(N-1)/2$, so $\sim N^2$. For large $N$ this is an awful lot of computation. Try to obtain a cheaper asymptotic scaling with $N$ if possible. If you can't, code an inefficient method scaling like $N^2$, for a partial credit of up to 13 pts.
