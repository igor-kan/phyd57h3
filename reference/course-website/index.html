Course PHYD57. Advanced Computational Methods in Physics

Welcome to the course that will help you become a more knowledgeable and better scientific programmer! You already know basic programming in Python (cf. next section). Now is the time to learn some history and even some physics behind our technical civilization, where computing is helping us everywhere. Powerful operating system (linux) and languages will be discussed and tried out, networking, and the principles of parallel programming that enables you as a scientist to run large-scale simulations of the physical world. We will also discuss some advanced algorithms used in Physics. The course includes practical exercises in writing and optimizing programs on single node(s) of a computational cluster. Although we'll outline the methods, we leave out the node-to-node communication and writing message-passing programs. You can acquire those skills later, as part of a research in undergrad or grad course.

Things highlighted in red on this page are newly added or important in the near future.

Languages

This course assumes you are interested in:
(i) Intermediate and advanced numerical methods used in physical sciences;
(ii) HPC (High Performance Computing), including parallelization of algorithms;
(iii) Coding in one of the HPC languages more powerful than Python.
After this course you will understand programs (with their arithmetic, iterations/loops, conditional statements, functions/subroutines and their calling, array declaration and usage, pseudorandom numbers, and so on) in both C and Fortran. You will be required to actively express your ideas (a more demanding task) in one of those languages, you choose which one. In other words, we want you to acquire passive knowledge of C(++) AND modern Fortran, and active knowledge of C(++) OR Fortran.
The pros and cons of C vs. Fortran will be discussed in the first meetings, but here is a brief take. By Fortran we mean modern fortran: 90, 95, 2003, 2008, or 2015 definitions; they are actually almost the same for our purposes. Avoid books/tutorials on really old, earlier FORTRAN versions (IV, 77), even though Fortran, unlike Python, is virtually backward compatible. Knowing Python, Fortran is easier to learn/use than C++. It has changed much since its inception long time ago, but one thing remained constant. From supercomputers to laptops, scientific simulations in Fortran (typically using multidimensional arrays) are among the fastest-executing. Which may be a surprise, because literally thousands of languages have been invented since Fortran. But very few with advanced physical computations in mind!

C(++) is also old, though a few decades younger, and different: more wordy, with more bells and whistles, it is lower level (we'll discuss what that means). It is better known and thus more marketable outside academia. C is a sine qua non for operating system writers, guardians and hackers. Many programmers are not happy with C++ (see the section with links on HPC languages below). Scientists have a love/hate relationship with C++; even though they weren't originally meant for each other, they've been together for ages and gave birth to many great implementations and program packages.

Intel and Nvidia are providing both C and Fortran in their separate, powerful compiler suites for free. And there are open-projects such as gcc and gfortran (every linux, macos & other OS has C/C++ compiler named gcc installed; g stands for GNU, which is an inside joke (ask your AI friend what GNU means).

Course contents

You have learned how to program (in PHYB57) and saw some basic numerical methods. Now it's time for more algorithms and techniques, used in numerical physics. You will discover the world of High Performance Computing (HPC): how and why it emerged in the history of computing. You will get to understand two HPC languages.

Practical skills will include a basic familiarity with the Linux operating system. If not familiar with it, you may want to read about it on the net even before the course starts. You are encouraged but not required to install some linux (Rocky, Red Hat, Fedora, Ubuntu, Mint or other flavor of Linux) on your computer (or use MacOS, which is basically Linux customized by Apple).
You will have access to one node of a cluster housed in BV building (there is a banner with a photo of the art cluster next to the entrance to DPES office in EV bldg). It has Centos 6 flavor of linux and the basic software, enough for doing exercises on your own remotely.
Physical access to the server room is restricted, even I don't have the codes and the key, so if the machine(s) crash because of something (e.g. power outage) and don't reboot correctly then we have a problem. Therefore you'll backup your work on your machine, as will I. As a disclaimer, making the research machine(s) available to you is outside the scope of u/g courses and is an extra benefit to you given by me without explicit or implicit promises. You should try to develop, debug and run codes on your own machine, it's not difficult.

On your computer, you will sometimes use your Python3, and will be asked to install compiler(s) for HPC languages Fortran and/or C(++), and familiarize yourself with how they function. (If you already know both these languages, which of course would be a nice surprise, then please try to install the Julia language compiler. Julia was born at MIT, for purposes consistent with ours. Sharing your experience will be appreciated - we'll think of exactly how once we get to that point.)

In summary, our goals are to help you become a better scientific programmer, able to solve numerically demanding problems on a workstation or a good laptop. While the practice of cluster supercomputing is outside the scope of this undergraduate course, you will hear a lot about it, and even have an option to try it small-scale if you want.

This is a 4th year (upper u/g) course. It explains the issues, tries to interest you in some directions and put you on the right track, but it's your job to move along. This means your own initiative in learning is expected. Lots of materials, books, web pages, will be suggested to you, and often you will make your own choice which of them to pursue closer. Start very soon. Happy experimenting with the kind of computing you likely haven't experienced yet.

• https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marian_Rejewski . Are you interested in ENIGMA and its decoding before and during WWII?
Then you have to dig deeper than the related Hollywood movies, which are at times extremely short on real history (better cf. Max Hastings, "The secret War: Spies, Codes and Guerillas 1939-1945", London, 2015). ENIGMA machines were used by Hitler's military for coded communications between the headquarters and units in the field. ENIGMA decoding, first achieved in Poland by Marian Rejewski's team, may eventually have preserved millions of lives, by saving England from German invasion, tipping the balance in favor of Allies, and shortening WWII.

After the pre-war Polish cryptologic breakthroughs, hardware devices were built there, implementing the decoding algorithms called Bomba (cryptographic bomb; or "bombe" in top-secret U.S. Army reports of the time). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bomba_(cryptography). Copies of ENIGMA machines were also built. Eventually an even faster hacking of the gradually more numerous combination wheels inside ENIGMAs became necessary.

This was organized by the British government using extension of the same approach as invented by the secret Polish Cipher Bureau. Electromechanical versions of the Polish Bomba were built in Blatchely Park near London by an outstanding pioneer of computational science Alan Turing. He also created some statistical theory to aid this work.
The recent biographical movie on A. Turing and the British Enigma-cracking effort titled "The imitation game" distorts the history, as the Turing's team is incorrectly given the sole credit for breaking ENIGMA code. In fact, they were neither the first nor the most amazing innovators. For instance, a half-forgotten team of brilliant engineers of the British Post Office headed by Thomas Flowers, working around the clock for months, constructed the specialized "Colossus" computer and transferred it to the Turing's bureau. That device is shown in the movie, but was neither Turing's invention nor tasked with deciphering the ENIGMA codes! It dealt with other German codes. It contained a super-fast correlator running two punched paper tape loops at 25 ft/s, and then doing arithmetic calculations that we estimate at 1 MFLOP rate (1 million floating point operations per second), way ahead of its time.

Even more powerful machines were developed during the WWII by American government (Navy), in order to decipher the changing coding procedure used by German submarines. Such devices were the first modern computers, though not general-purpose electronic computers we have today. They were mostly electromechanical, that is to say, based on relays and automatic switches.

As you see, computing from the beginning was and continues to be very very useful for some people. Unfortunately these days it is often used for spying on... you (and everyone with a smartphone). Anyway, enjoy the four ENIGMAtic movies nicely broken down for you on this page .

Connectivity (ciphers) to login to the cluster

Try using these options to ssh, creating an alias like so (it's not important but you can change 38 to some other number between, say, 30 and 90, so we don't ping the machines at the same time interval):
alias ssh='ssh -X -o TCPKeepAlive=no -o ServerAliveInterval=38 -o HostKeyAlgorithms=+ssh-dss'

Navier-Stokes blow-up (Millenium Problem) problem solved on September 8, 2026?

We will spend a few moments talking about the Millenium prize problem involving the Navier-Stokes equation blow-up in finite time, supposedly just solved on September 8, 2026 by company OpenAI, using lots of graphics cards (GPUs). Since nobody yet seems to understand the 160+ page math proof, even after asking (I'm sure many tried) AI to summarize it, for now you can only listen to discussions of a feud this already caused among the AI teams like Anthropic and OpenAI, such as this: The Controversy Behind AI Solving A Million Dollar Math Problem.
